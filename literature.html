Literature Review

Research on scuba diving fatalities, especially in reports published by the Divers Alert Network (DAN), provides a strong descriptive foundation for understanding fatal incidents. Across annual DAN reports, fatalities are consistently described as multifactorial involving diver health, environmental stressors, and situational aspects. Variables such as age, BMI, pre-existing medical conditions, dive depth, and environmental conditions are frequently documented. For example, DAN data indicates that a large share of fatalities involve older divers and individuals classified as overweight or obese, suggesting that cardiovascular strain and overall fitness are important contributing factors. Depth is also repeatedly identified as a key variable, since deeper dives increase physiological stress, air-consumption rates, and ascents, all of which elevate risk.

While these variables are well documented, most existing research relies on descriptive statistics such as counts and proportions by category. As a result, the literature clearly identifies which factors are present in fatal incidents, but offers limited insight into how these factors interact or how risk changes over time. For instance, although BMI and depth are each associated with fatalities, few studies examine whether higher BMI specifically amplifies risk during deeper or more physically demanding dives. Likewise, while DAN reports span multiple years, limited work has analyzed trends in whether fatalities involving older divers or particular dive conditions are increasing over time.

The structure of DAN datasets allows for more advanced statistical investigation of these gaps. Using incident-level data across multiple years, it is possible to examine trends in key variables, including changes in average diver age, BMI distribution, or depth of fatal dives. In addition, multivariate statistical methods, including logistic regression and modeling, can test whether combinations of variables are more strongly associated with fatalities than individual factors alone. This shifts analysis toward a more complete understanding of risk.

Another limitation in the literature is the lack of comparison between fatal and non-fatal incidents, which restricts the ability to identify when risk escalates. DAN reports often describe chains of events leading to death, but these sequences are not typically quantified or modeled statistically. With data structuring, it becomes possible to analyze patterns in contributing factors and investigate which combinations of initial conditions are most likely to result in fatal outcomes.

Overall, existing research establishes that scuba diving fatalities are rare, complex events influenced by multiple variables, but it does not fully leverage the analytical potential of available datasets. This project addresses those gaps by using DAN data to investigate interaction effects, temporal trends, and multivariate relationships among variables such as BMI, depth, and diver demographics. In doing so, it aims to improve understanding of how risk develops and changes over time and to identify patterns not captured in traditional descriptive analysis.
