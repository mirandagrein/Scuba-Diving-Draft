Literature

Literature Review

Examining existing research on diving safety and the gaps this project addresses

Research on scuba diving fatalities, especially in reports published by the Divers Alert Network (DAN), provides a strong foundation for understanding fatal incidents. Across annual DAN reports, fatalities are consistently described as multifactorial involving diver health, environmental stressors, and situational aspects. Variables such as age, BMI, pre-existing medical conditions, dive depth, and environmental conditions are frequently documented.

What We Know and What We Do Not Know

Existing sources cited in this project provide strong context from different angles. DAN annual reports provide incident reports, Lippmann and Caruso provide forensic and context for diver cause of death, and Lucrezi et al. emphasize operational safety priorities in recreational settings. These works support the conclusion that fatalities are multi-causal and pathway-based rather than caused by one variable alone.

What remains less developed in the literature is pathway development across stages of an incident. Much of the evidence is still reported as category percentages, for example how often one outcome appears, but fewer studies model how triggers transition into mechanisms, then disabling injuries, and then final causes of death over time. That is the key aspect this project addresses and investigates.

While these variables are well documented, most existing research still relies on descriptive statistics such as counts and proportions by category. In practice, many summaries stop at statements like “most deaths were drowning” or “a certain percentage involved a given factor,” which is useful but limited. This approach identifies what appears frequently, but it does not explain how factors connect across a fatal sequence or how those relationships shift across years. Likewise, while DAN reports span multiple years, limited work has quantified whether specific pathway patterns are becoming more or less common over time.

The structure of DAN datasets allows for more advanced statistical investigation of these gaps. Using incident-level data across multiple years, it is possible to examine trends in key variables, including changes in variables. Multivariate statistical methods can test whether combinations of variables are more strongly associated with fatalities than individual factors alone. This shifts analysis toward a more complete understanding of risk. This project moves beyond isolated percentages and evaluates which combinations of conditions repeatedly converge into fatal outcomes.

Overall, existing research establishes that scuba diving fatalities are complex events influenced by multiple variables, but it does not fully leverage the potential of available datasets for analysis. This project addresses those gaps by combining descriptive trend work with pathway-based network analysis of DAN data to investigate these relationships. The aim is to improve understanding of how risk develops and changes over time and to identify relevant prevention techniques.
